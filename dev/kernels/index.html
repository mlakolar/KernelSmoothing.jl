Kernels

A kernel $K$ enters an estimator through the weights $K((x_i - x)/h)/h$, where $h > 0$ is the bandwidth. The package provides six second-order kernels, that is, symmetric probability densities with a finite and positive second moment.

kernel$K(u)$ on its support$\mu_2(K)$$R(K)$smoothness
UniformKernel$1/2$$1/3$$1/2$0
TriangularKernel$1 - \lvert u \rvert$$1/6$$2/3$1
EpanechnikovKernel$\tfrac{3}{4} (1 - u^2)$$1/5$$3/5$1
BiweightKernel$\tfrac{15}{16} (1 - u^2)^2$$1/7$$5/7$2
TriweightKernel$\tfrac{35}{32} (1 - u^2)^3$$1/9$$350/429$3
GaussianKernel$\phi(u) = e^{-u^2/2}/\sqrt{2\pi}$$1$$1/(2\sqrt{\pi})$$\infty$

Here $\mu_2(K) = \int u^2 K(u)\,du$ is the variance of the kernel and $R(K) = \int K(u)^2\,du$ its roughness. The compactly supported kernels live on $[-1, 1]$, so that $h$ is the half-width of the smoothing window, as in nprobust, rdrobust and lpdensity. The Gaussian kernel is the standard normal density, so that its bandwidth is a standard deviation.

Every kernel can be evaluated as k(u) and supports the following functions.

Several packages export a function named derivative, Polynomials.jl among them. With one of them loaded as well, Julia asks for the name to be qualified, as in KernelSmoothing.derivative(k, 2). The same holds for GaussianKernel and PiecewisePolynomialKernel with KernelFunctions.jl, whose kernels of the same names are covariance functions of Gaussian processes. Since support(k) is a tuple, u in support(k) compares u with the ends; insupport(k, u) tests membership.

Since derivatives, convolutions and products are kernels again, the estimators treat them like any other kernel. A kernel has smoothness $s$ if $K \in C^{s-1}$ and $K^{(s)}$ exists as a function, possibly with jumps; then $K^{(r)}$ for $r \leq s$ is the derivative in the sense of distributions, and derivative(k, r) refuses larger orders, whose distributional derivatives have point masses. Integration by parts gives the moments of a derivative,

\[\int u^j K^{(r)}(u)\,du = (-1)^r \frac{j!}{(j - r)!}\, \mu_{j-r}(K), \qquad j \geq r .\]

The compactly supported kernels and their derivatives, convolutions and products are polynomials between breakpoints, which PiecewisePolynomialKernel(breaks, coefs) also lets the user define, for example higher-order kernels. Each piece is stored as a polynomial about its midpoint, which keeps evaluation accurate far from zero, and convolutions and products are computed exactly in this representation. The named kernels are evaluated as powers of $1 - u^2$ and their distribution functions near $-1$ as $(1 + u)^m P(u)$, with the terms of $P$ of one sign, since the expanded polynomials cancel near $\pm 1$ and can turn negative there. For the same reason, a piecewise polynomial kernel keeps its distribution function on the first piece and its upper tail on the last piece as polynomials about the ends of the support, computed in extended precision from the exact pieces of a convolution or a product, so that both keep their relative accuracy at the ends. Derivatives, convolutions and products of Gaussian kernels stay in the family

\[K_{\sigma, r, a}(u) = \frac{a}{\sigma^{r+1}}\, \phi^{(r)}\Big(\frac{u}{\sigma}\Big), \qquad \phi^{(r)}(z) = (-1)^r \mathrm{He}_r(z)\, \phi(z),\]

where $\mathrm{He}_r$ is the probabilists' Hermite polynomial of degree $r$; a convolution adds the variances and the orders.

KernelSmoothing.AbstractKernel — Type
AbstractKernel

Supertype of smoothing kernels. A kernel k is a function of a scaled distance: an estimator with bandwidth h gives an observation at distance d the weight k(d / h) / h.

The named kernels are symmetric probability densities with a finite, positive second moment, that is, second-order kernels. The compactly supported ones live on [-1, 1], so h is the half-width of the smoothing window. GaussianKernel is the standard normal density, for which h is a standard deviation. A bandwidth h_sd in the standard-deviation convention of KernelDensity.jl, KDEpy and R's density corresponds to the half-width h = h_sd / sqrt(moment(k, 2)).

Every kernel can be evaluated, k(u), and supports support, cdf, ccdf, moment, roughness, smoothness, derivative and convolve. Derivatives and convolutions are kernels too, although a derivative integrates to zero rather than one.

A new kernel type K <: AbstractKernel defines the evaluation (k::K)(u::Real) and support(k), which kernel_sums and local polynomial regression with a given bandwidth need. A kernel density estimate also needs moment(k, j), to check that the kernel integrates to one and for its mean and variance, and cdf(k, u) for its distribution function and quantiles; ccdf(k, u) keeps its upper tail accurate where moment(k, 0) - cdf(k, u) cancels. The plug-in rules and the rules of thumb need moments of k and k² and roughness(k); the last two are computed by quadrature unless the type defines them. derivative and convolve, for derivatives of densities and least-squares cross-validation, are available for the polynomial and Gaussian kernels. The compactly supported kernels of LocalPolynomialDensity must be polynomial kernels, such as a PiecewisePolynomialKernel.

BiweightKernel()

The biweight, or quartic, kernel k(u) = 15/16 (1 - u^2)^2 on [-1, 1].

KernelSmoothing.EpanechnikovKernel — Type
EpanechnikovKernel()

The Epanechnikov kernel k(u) = 3/4 (1 - u^2) on [-1, 1], which minimizes the asymptotic mean integrated squared error among second-order kernels.

Examples

julia> k = EpanechnikovKernel();

julia> k(0.5)
0.5625

julia> moment(k, 2), roughness(k)
(0.2, 0.6)

KernelSmoothing.GaussianKernel — Type
GaussianKernel()

The Gaussian kernel k(u) = exp(-u^2/2) / sqrt(2π), the standard normal density. It has unbounded support, so the bandwidth is its standard deviation, and derivatives of every order.

source
KernelSmoothing.PiecewisePolynomialKernel — Type
PiecewisePolynomialKernel(breaks, coefs)

A compactly supported kernel that is a polynomial between consecutive breakpoints: on [breaks[j], breaks[j+1]] it equals coefs[j][1] + coefs[j][2] u + coefs[j][3] u^2 + ..., and it is zero outside [breaks[1], breaks[end]]. derivative and convolve return this type for polynomial kernels. It also defines new kernels, for example the fourth-order kernel 3/8 (3 - 5u^2) on [-1, 1]:

julia> k = KernelSmoothing.PiecewisePolynomialKernel([-1, 1], [[9/8, 0, -15/8]]);

julia> moment(k, 0), moment(k, 2)
(1.0, 0.0)

The smoothness is found by checking which derivatives are continuous at the breakpoints. KernelFunctions.jl exports a different type of the same name; when both packages are loaded, qualify this one as KernelSmoothing.PiecewisePolynomialKernel.

The coefficients are converted in 256-bit precision, set with setprecision, which is local to the task from Julia 1.11 on but global on Julia 1.10; there, building kernels while another task changes the BigFloat precision is unsafe.

Base.:* — Method
k1 * k2

The product u ↦ k1(u) k2(u) of two kernels, as a kernel. Variances need k * k, because the variance of a kernel estimate is a sum of wᵢ² k(uᵢ)² terms. Products of polynomial kernels are polynomial kernels on the intersection of the supports, computed in 256-bit precision; products of Gaussian kernels without derivatives are scaled Gaussian kernels. Other products throw an ArgumentError.

Distributions.ccdf — Method
ccdf(k::AbstractKernel, u)

The integral of the kernel k from u to Inf, moment(k, 0) - cdf(k, u), computed without the cancellation of that difference where the upper tail is small. A new kernel type can define it to keep its upper tail accurate, which the upper tail of a KDE with the kernel then inherits; otherwise the difference is used.

source
Distributions.cdf — Method
cdf(k::AbstractKernel, u)

The integral of the kernel k from -Inf to u. For a named kernel this is a distribution function. For a derivative of order r ≥ 1 it is the derivative of order r - 1.

source
Distributions.convolve — Method
convolve(k1::AbstractKernel, k2::AbstractKernel)

The convolution u ↦ ∫ k1(u - v) k2(v) dv, as a kernel. Least-squares cross-validation needs convolve(k, k), because ∫ f̂(x)^2 dx is a double sum of it over pairs of observations. Convolutions of polynomial kernels are polynomial kernels, and convolutions of Gaussian kernels and their derivatives are derivatives of scaled Gaussian densities. Mixing the two families throws an ArgumentError. Polynomial convolutions are computed in 256-bit precision, with the caveat for Julia 1.10 described under PiecewisePolynomialKernel.

source
Distributions.insupport — Method
insupport(k::AbstractKernel, u)

Whether u lies in the support (lo, hi) of the kernel k, that is, lo ≤ u ≤ hi.

source
Distributions.support — Method
support(k::AbstractKernel)

The interval (lo, hi) outside which the kernel k is zero, as a tuple: (-1.0, 1.0) for the compactly supported named kernels and (-Inf, Inf) for the Gaussian. Since it is a tuple, u in support(k) asks whether u equals one of the ends; test membership with insupport(k, u). (The support of a KDE, a distribution, is an interval, for which in works.)

source
KernelSmoothing.derivative — Function
derivative(k, r = 1)

The derivative of order r of the kernel k, as a kernel. The estimate ∑_i derivative(k, r)((x - X_i) / h) / (n h^(r + 1)) is the r-th derivative of the kernel density estimate.

Throws an ArgumentError when r exceeds smoothness(k). The Epanechnikov kernel, for example, has a first derivative but no second, because its first derivative jumps at ±1.

Other packages, such as Polynomials.jl, export a derivative of their own; with one of them loaded, qualify the name as KernelSmoothing.derivative.

source
KernelSmoothing.roughness — Function
roughness(k)

The integral ∫ k(u)^2 du. A kernel density estimate with bandwidth h has variance close to roughness(k) f(x) / (n h). Plug-in bandwidth selectors also need the roughness of a derivative, roughness(derivative(k, r)).

source
KernelSmoothing.smoothness — Function
smoothness(k)

The number of derivatives of the kernel k that are functions. For r ≤ smoothness(k), the derivative of order r exists almost everywhere and is the derivative in the sense of distributions, so derivative(k, r) estimates the r-th derivative of a density. The next derivative has point masses where the kernel or one of its derivatives jumps.

source
StatsBase.moment — Method
moment(k::AbstractKernel, j)

The moment ∫ u^j k(u) du of order j ≥ 0. A second-order kernel has moment(k, 0) == 1, moment(k, 1) == 0 and a positive moment(k, 2), its variance.
