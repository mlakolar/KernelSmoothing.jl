Bandwidth selection

Estimators accept a number or a BandwidthSelector as bandwidth. select_bandwidth applies a rule and returns a BandwidthSelection that records what the rule computed, such as pilot bandwidths or a cross-validation curve, and every fit stores the selection that chose its bandwidth.

For densities, SheatherJones is the default; LSCV, NormalReference and SilvermanRule are alternatives. For regression, the default follows nprobust: MSEOptimal at a single point and IMSEOptimal at several, the plug-in bandwidths of Calonico, Cattaneo and Farrell (2019), with pilot bandwidths anchored on cross-validation. CoverageOptimal rescales them for robust bias-corrected inference, FLCIOptimal chooses bandwidths for honest intervals, and RuleOfThumb and CrossValidation are alternatives. For regression discontinuity, RDOptimal gives the plug-in bandwidths of rdrobust; see Regression discontinuity.

d = SmoothingData(randn(1000))
s = select_bandwidth(LSCV(), d, EpanechnikovKernel())
s.h, s.details.minimizers        # the bandwidth, and all local minimizers of the criterion

Rules of thumb

NormalReference gives the bandwidth that minimizes the asymptotic mean integrated squared error of a kernel density estimate when the density is normal,

\[h = \hat\sigma \left( \frac{8 \sqrt{\pi}\, R(K)}{3 \mu_2(K)^2\, n} \right)^{1/5},\]

with $R(K) = \int K^2$, $\mu_2(K) = \int u^2 K(u)\,du$ and $\hat\sigma$ the smaller of the standard deviation and the interquartile range divided by 1.349. This is $1.06\, \hat\sigma n^{-1/5}$ for the Gaussian kernel and $2.34\, \hat\sigma n^{-1/5}$ for the Epanechnikov kernel. SilvermanRule is R's bw.nrd0. Weighted samples use Kish's effective sample size for $n$.

For regression, RuleOfThumb is the rule of Fan and Gijbels (1996, Section 4.2),

\[h = C_{\nu,p}(K) \left( \frac{\tilde\sigma^2 (\max_i x_i - \min_i x_i)}{n D} \right)^{1/(2p+3)},\]

where the residual variance $\tilde\sigma^2$ and the mean squared derivative $D$ of order $p + 1$ come from a global polynomial fit of order $p + 3$ in the standardized covariate, and $C_{\nu,p}(K)$ depends on the equivalent kernel.

Sheather and Jones

The rule of Sheather and Jones (1991) estimates $\psi_4 = \int f''(x)^2\,dx$ with a Gaussian pilot kernel and solves

\[h = \left( \frac{R(K)}{\mu_2(K)^2\, \hat\psi_4(\alpha_2 h^{5/7})\, n} \right)^{1/5},\]

where $\alpha_2$ comes from estimates of $\psi_4$ and $\psi_6$ at normal-reference pilot bandwidths. The estimates are the double sums

\[\hat\psi_r(g) = \frac{1}{W^2 - \sum_i w_i^2} \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^n w_i w_j\, \phi_g^{(r)}(x_i - x_j),\]

with the diagonal included, which with equal weights is the definition of R's bw.SJ. They are computed exactly for up to 100 observations. Larger samples are binned linearly once, on a grid with about 100 points per pilot bandwidth, and the lagged products of the bin weights come from an FFT, which changes the bandwidth by about $10^{-6}$ relative. Far outliers and heavy tails would need too fine a grid over the whole range: the data are then split at gaps across which the kernels are negligible, only the dense part of each run is binned, and the pairs with the remaining observations, which are sparse, are summed exactly. The equation is solved to a relative accuracy of $10^{-10}$. For other kernels than the Gaussian, the bandwidth is the Gaussian one times the ratio $(R(K)/\mu_2(K)^2)^{1/5}$ of canonical bandwidths. With method = :dpi, the pilot bandwidth is the direct plug-in instead of $\alpha_2 h^{5/7}$. The rule is the recommended default for densities without boundaries (Jones, Marron and Sheather 1996).

Cross-validation

Least-squares cross-validation, LSCV, minimizes an unbiased estimate of the integrated squared error minus $\int f^2$,

\[\mathrm{CV}(h) = \int \hat f(x)^2\,dx - \frac{2}{W^2 - \sum_i w_i^2} \sum_{i \neq j} w_i w_j\, \frac{1}{h} K\Big(\frac{x_i - x_j}{h}\Big),\]

on a grid of bandwidths spaced evenly on the log scale, whose lower end comes from a robust scale estimate, so that outliers do not lift it. The criterion often has several local minimizers. Following Sheather (2004), the rule prefers the largest, since spurious ones appear at small bandwidths, as for rounded data, where the criterion falls without bound as $h$ shrinks. With compact kernels, however, clustered data give local minimizers at the scale of the gaps between the clusters, where the criterion is far above its lowest local minimum. The rule therefore takes the largest local minimizer whose criterion is within half of the lowest one, and refines it. Where the criterion is lowest at the lower end of the grid and the data have no ties, the grid extends downward first. For regression, CrossValidation minimizes

\[\mathrm{CV}(h) = \frac{\sum_i w_i \big(y_i - \hat m_{-i}(x_i)\big)^2}{\sum_i w_i}, \qquad \hat m_{-i}(x_i) = \frac{\hat m(x_i) - \ell_{ii} y_i}{1 - \ell_{ii}},\]

where $\ell_{ii}$ is the weight of $y_i$ in the fit at $x_i$. The fits at all observations come from kernel_sums, so that a bandwidth costs $O(n)$ operations for polynomial kernels; for Gaussian kernels and more than 1000 observations, the sums come from binned data where that is faster. The grid starts where the leave-one-out fits exist, at the smallest bandwidth that leaves $p + 1$ other distinct values in the window of each observation. Isolated observations, whose distance to their $(p+1)$-th nearest other distinct value is more than three interquartile ranges above the upper quartile of these distances, would set this floor for all, so the criterion leaves them out and the sums run over the others. The rule takes the global minimizer over the grid, refines it between its neighbours, and warns when it lies at the lower end of the grid. Cross-validation assumes independent errors; with positively correlated errors it undersmooths.

Plug-in bandwidths for regression

At a point, the mean squared error of the estimate of $m^{(\nu)}$ by a fit of order $p$ is approximately $h^{2(p+1-\nu)} B^2 + V/(n h^{1+2\nu})$. Here $B = C_B \hat\beta_{p+1}$ is the bias, with $\hat\beta_{p+1}$ an estimate of the next Taylor coefficient, and $V$ the variance, both with the finite-sample constants of the local fits. MSEOptimal minimizes it at each point,

\[h = \left( \frac{(1 + 2\nu)\, V}{2 (p + 1 - \nu)\, n\, (B^2 + \lambda R)} \right)^{1/(2p+3)},\]

where $R = 3 C_B^2 \hat V[\hat\beta_{p+1}]$ regularizes the bandwidth where the derivative nearly vanishes, scaled by regularization $= \lambda$. When $p - \nu$ is even, the bias includes its next term and the error is minimized numerically, with the squared bias $(|B_1| + h |B_2|)^2 + \lambda R$; nprobust's $(B_1 + h B_2 + \lambda R)^2$, which compat = true keeps, vanishes where the estimated terms cancel, and its bandwidth depends on the units of $y$. The derivative in $B$ is estimated by a fit of order $p + 1$ with a pilot bandwidth $b$. By default, $b$ is the cross-validation bandwidth of the fit of order $p$ times the ratio of the rule-of-thumb bandwidths for the derivative of order $p + 1$ and for the regression function. Since cross-validation adapts to the roughness of the regression function, so does the pilot. With compat = true, the pilots come from the stages of nprobust's lpbwselect, whose bias constants are estimated by fits over the whole range of the data. At interior points these stages end at their cap, the distance to the farther end of the data, so that the derivative is estimated over nearly the whole range and the bandwidths are too large where the curvature is local.

IMSEOptimal averages the variance and squared-bias constants over a grid, 30 quantiles of the covariate of order 0.05 to 0.95, or the evaluation points with grid = :points, and gives one bandwidth, at most the range of the data. nprobust, whose rule compat = true reproduces, averages over 30 equally spaced points between the smallest and the largest observation, which for a covariate with heavy tails lie mostly where few observations are left, and its squared bias leaves out the regularization. CoverageOptimal rescales the plug-in bandwidths by a power of the sample size, which makes the coverage error of robust bias-corrected intervals shrink at the fastest rate (Calonico, Cattaneo and Farrell 2018, 2022). For odd $p - \nu$ this rate differs between interior and boundary points. By default the rule takes the interior rate, which leaves local linear bandwidths unchanged, and compat = true the boundary rate at every point, as nprobust. The same rules give the bandwidths of the density estimators; see Density estimation.

KernelSmoothing.BandwidthSelector — Type
BandwidthSelector

Supertype of the rules that choose a bandwidth from the data, such as NormalReference and SilvermanRule. select_bandwidth applies a rule and records what it computed on the way, and bandwidth returns only the bandwidth. Estimators accept a rule wherever they accept a bandwidth.

A new rule R <: BandwidthSelector implements select_bandwidth(rule::R, d::SmoothingData, k::AbstractKernel).

KernelSmoothing.NormalReference — Type
NormalReference(; robust = true)

The bandwidth that minimizes the asymptotic mean integrated squared error when the density is normal,

h = σ̂ (8 √π R(k) / (3 μ₂(k)² n))^(1/5),

where R(k) = roughness(k), μ₂(k) = moment(k, 2) and n is the effective_nobs. The scale σ̂ is the standard deviation or, with robust = true, the smaller of the standard deviation and the interquartile range divided by 1.349, which guards against heavy tails and outliers. For the Gaussian kernel the rule gives 1.06 σ̂ n^(-1/5) and for the Epanechnikov kernel 2.34 σ̂ n^(-1/5).

The rule oversmooths densities that are far from normal, multimodal ones in particular. It is a quick default and a pilot for better selectors. It needs a second-order kernel, with moment(k, 2) > 0.

source
KernelSmoothing.SilvermanRule — Type
SilvermanRule()

Silverman's rule of thumb, 0.9 min(σ̂, IQR / 1.34) n^(-1/5) for the Gaussian kernel, where σ̂ is the standard deviation, IQR the interquartile range and n the effective_nobs. It is the default bandwidth of R's density (bw.nrd0) and of KernelDensity.jl. Other kernels get the bandwidth that R's density uses for them, the Gaussian one divided by the standard deviation of the kernel, sqrt(moment(k, 2)). Compared with NormalReference, the factor 0.9 instead of 1.06 suits densities with several modes better.

KernelSmoothing.CrossValidation — Type
CrossValidation(; gridsize = 40, exact = 1000)

Leave-one-out cross-validation for local polynomial regression: the bandwidth that minimizes

CV(h) = ∑ᵢ wᵢ (yᵢ - m̂₋ᵢ(xᵢ))² / ∑ᵢ wᵢ,   m̂₋ᵢ(xᵢ) = (m̂(xᵢ) - ℓᵢᵢ yᵢ) / (1 - ℓᵢᵢ),

where m̂₋ᵢ(xᵢ) is the fit at xᵢ without observation i and ℓᵢᵢ the weight of yᵢ in the fit at xᵢ. The fits at all observations come from kernel_sums, so that a bandwidth costs O(n) operations for polynomial kernels. Gaussian kernels use exact sums for up to exact observations, and otherwise, where it is faster, the data binned linearly on a grid of 50 points per standard deviation of the kernel, with the sums from FFT convolutions; this changes the criterion by about 1e-5 relative, which moves the bandwidth by less than its refinement tolerance.

The grid has gridsize bandwidths spaced evenly on the log scale, from the smallest one that leaves p + 1 other distinct values in the window of each scored observation, below which leave-one-out fits break down, to the range of the data, or to twice that smallest one if it is larger. The criterion scores every observation except isolated ones, whose distance to their (p + 1)-th nearest other distinct value is far out among these distances, more than three interquartile ranges above the upper quartile; they still enter the fits of the others. A single isolated observation used to set the floor of the grid for all: one x at 10 among 1000 uniform ones moved the default regression bandwidth from 0.08 to 4.7. The global minimizer on the grid is refined to a relative accuracy of 1e-3, far below the statistical error of the bandwidth, and the rule warns when it lies at the lower end of the grid. The details record the grid, the criterion and the number of scored observations. Cross-validation estimates the regression function, so it needs derivative = 0.

KernelSmoothing.LSCV — Type
LSCV(; gridsize = 60, exact = 1000)

Least-squares cross-validation for kernel density estimates (Rudemo 1982; Bowman 1984). It minimizes an unbiased estimate of the integrated squared error minus ∫ f²,

CV(h) = ∫ f̂(x)² dx - 2 ∑_{i ≠ j} wᵢ wⱼ k((xᵢ - xⱼ) / h) / (h (W² - ∑ᵢ wᵢ²)),

over gridsize bandwidths spaced evenly on the log scale between h_OS / 100 and 4 h_OS, where h_OS is the oversmoothed bandwidth of Terrell (1990), an upper bound for the bandwidth that minimizes the asymptotic mean integrated squared error. The minimizer of the criterion itself can exceed h_OS, as it does for normal data about half of the time, hence the factor 4. The lower end uses the robust scale of NormalReference, so that outliers and heavy tails, which inflate the standard deviation, do not lift the whole grid. Following Sheather (2004), the rule prefers the largest local minimizer, since the criterion often has spurious minimizers at small bandwidths: with rounded data, it falls without bound as h shrinks. With compact kernels, however, clustered data give local minimizers at the scale of the gaps between the clusters, where the criterion is far above its lowest local minimum; for two normal samples 10 apart, the largest minimizer was at 13.3, with a criterion of -0.045 against -0.126 at 0.95. The rule therefore takes the largest local minimizer whose criterion is within half of the lowest local minimum, and refines it. Where the criterion is lowest at the lower end of the grid and the data have no ties, the grid first extends downward by up to three decades, as for lognormal data, whose minimizer lay at a fifth of the lower end. The details of the selection record the grid, the criterion, all local minimizers and whether the choice was at an end of the grid, and the rule warns then.

Polynomial kernels use exact fast sums. Gaussian kernels use exact double sums for up to exact observations and otherwise the data binned once, on a grid of 100 points per smallest bandwidth, with the lagged products of the bin weights, as in SheatherJones, or binned again for each bandwidth where that grid would need more than 2^20 points; binning moves the bandwidth by about 1e-5 relative. Cross-validation converges slowly, at the relative rate n^(-1/10), and varies a lot between samples, so it is an alternative to SheatherJones rather than a default.

KernelSmoothing.SheatherJones — Type
SheatherJones(; method = :ste, exact = 100, compat = false)

The plug-in bandwidth of Sheather and Jones (1991) for kernel density estimates. It estimates ψ₄ = ∫ f''(x)² dx with a Gaussian pilot kernel and plugs the estimate into the bandwidth that minimizes the asymptotic mean integrated squared error,

h = (R(k) / (μ₂(k)² ψ̂₄(g) n))^(1/5),

where R(k) = roughness(k), μ₂(k) = moment(k, 2) and n is the effective_nobs. With method = :ste, "solve the equation", the pilot bandwidth g = α₂ h^(5/7) depends on h itself, with α₂ estimated from ψ̂₄ and ψ̂₆ at normal-reference pilot bandwidths, and the equation is solved to a relative accuracy of 1e-10, or 1e-8 from binned data. With method = :dpi, the direct plug-in, g is the bandwidth that is optimal for estimating ψ₄ when ψ₆ is estimated at its normal-reference pilot bandwidth.

The estimates are the double sums of Sheather and Jones,

ψ̂ᵣ(g) = ∑ᵢ ∑ⱼ wᵢ wⱼ φ_g⁽ʳ⁾(xᵢ - xⱼ) / (W² - ∑ᵢ wᵢ²),   W = ∑ᵢ wᵢ,

with the diagonal included, which with equal weights is the definition of R's bw.SJ. They are computed exactly for up to exact observations and otherwise from the data binned linearly once, on a grid with about 100 points per smallest pilot bandwidth, with the lagged products of the bin weights from an FFT. This changes the estimates by about 1e-5 and the bandwidth by about 1e-6 relative, far below its statistical error, and makes the selection take a few milliseconds for 10^6 observations of a smooth density; heavy tails take longer, since their sparse stretches are summed exactly. Data that would need more than 2^20 grid points, because of far outliers or heavy tails, are split at gaps across which the pilot kernels are negligible, and each run into stretches of at most 2^18 grid points, of which the densest one and the others that are dense enough are binned; the pairs with the other observations, which are sparse, and the pairs across the boundaries of binned stretches are summed exactly. R's bw.SJ bins differences of pairs of observations coarsely and stops its search early, so by default it agrees with this rule to two or three digits only. With fine bins it agrees to about 1e-6 for method = :dpi and 4e-5 for :ste, where R rounds the constant (2 φ⁽⁴⁾(0) μ₂(k)² / R(k))^(1/7) = 1.35733 of the Gaussian kernel to 1.357. This rule is the recommended default for densities without boundaries (Jones, Marron and Sheather 1996).

With rounded data, pairs of equal values make ψ̂₄(g) grow like g⁻⁵ as the pilot bandwidth shrinks, and the equation gains roots at bandwidths that resolve the grid of the values: for 10⁵ normal observations rounded to 0.1, the roots were 0.0026, 0.017 and 0.106, and R's bw.SJ takes the smallest, at which the estimate is a comb of spikes. The rule therefore searches only bandwidths with h sd(k) ≥ r/2, where sd(k) = √μ₂(k) and r is the median gap between neighbouring distinct values, which for data that are not rounded is of the order of their range over n and changes nothing. Where no root is left, or where the direct plug-in falls below this bound, the normal-reference bandwidth is used, raised to the bound if it is smaller, with a warning; for integers with a standard deviation of 2, the normal reference alone gave 0.25 for the Gaussian kernel, which still resolves the integers.

compat = true gives R's bw.SJ exactly, as density(x, bw = "SJ", kernel = …) uses it: the bandwidth for the Gaussian kernel, from R's binned pair counts, rounded constants and root search, divided by √μ₂(k) for other kernels, so that the kernel has the standard deviation of R's bandwidth. Like bw.SJ, it takes no weights and throws where bw.SJ stops. For data far from zero relative to their range, beyond about 2 × 10⁶ times the range (2³¹ bins of 1.01/1000 of it), R 4.5's integer bins overflow and put every pair at distance zero; this rule bins from the minimum, as R's development sources do.

KernelSmoothing.CoverageOptimal — Type
CoverageOptimal(base = MSEOptimal(); convention = nothing)

Bandwidths that make the coverage error of robust bias-corrected intervals shrink at the fastest rate, obtained by rescaling the plug-in bandwidths of base (MSEOptimal or IMSEOptimal) by a power of the sample size (Calonico, Cattaneo and Farrell 2018, 2022).

With convention = :nprobust, the rescaling targets the rate n^(-1/(p+3)), which is coverage-error optimal at boundary points and for even p - ν, at every point: h n^(-p/((2p+3)(p+3))) for odd p - ν and h n^(-(p+2)/((2p+5)(p+3))) for even p - ν, as nprobust's ce-rot. With convention = :interior, an odd p - ν targets the interior rate n^(-1/(p+4)) instead, h n^(-(p-1)/((2p+3)(p+4))), which leaves local linear bandwidths unchanged. The pilot bandwidths are rescaled as in nprobust in both conventions. By default the convention is :interior, since most evaluation points are interior ones, and :nprobust with compat = true.

KernelSmoothing.IMSEOptimal — Type
IMSEOptimal(; grid = nothing, regularization = 1, interior = false)

The direct plug-in estimate of the bandwidth that minimizes the integrated mean squared error of a local polynomial fit, one bandwidth for all points: the variance and squared bias constants of MSEOptimal, with its regularization, are averaged over a grid of points, and the bandwidth is capped at the range of the data. For regression, the grid is by default 30 quantiles of the covariate, of order 0.05 to 0.95, so that the average estimates the error at a typical observation; grid = m takes m such quantiles, and grid = :points the evaluation points of the fit. nprobust's imse-dpi, which compat = true reproduces, averages over equally spaced points between the smallest and the largest observation. For a covariate with heavy tails these lie mostly where few observations are left, and the constants estimated there, which are large and unstable, dominate the average. The pilot bandwidths are those of MSEOptimal, adaptive by default, and b is in details.b. With compat = true the rule is nprobust's, whose pilots are its own, whose averaged squared bias leaves out the regularization and whose bandwidth has no cap, which for an exactly linear regression function was 966 on the unit interval.

For a sample without responses, the rule gives the integrated plug-in bandwidth for densities, with the constants of MSEOptimal averaged by default over 30 quantiles of order 0.1 to 0.9, as in nprobust's kdbwselect, which compat = true reproduces; grid = m takes m such quantiles. For LocalPolynomialDensity, it is lpdensity's imse-dpi, which sums the terms of MSEOptimal by default over the evaluation points, as lpdensity does, and with grid = m over m equally spaced points between the smallest and the largest observation. grid = :points needs evaluation points.

KernelSmoothing.MSEOptimal — Type
MSEOptimal(; regularization = 1, interior = false)

The direct plug-in estimate of the bandwidth that minimizes the mean squared error of a local polynomial fit at each evaluation point (Calonico, Cattaneo and Farrell 2019). At each point, the derivative of order p + 1 that drives the bias of the fit of order p is estimated by a fit of order q = p + 1 with the pilot bandwidth b, and h minimizes the estimated mean squared error, with the finite-sample bias and variance constants of the local fits, a preliminary normal-reference bandwidth for the variances, a cap at the distance to the farther end of the data and a floor at the distance of the minobs-th nearest observation. The squared bias B₁² carries the regularization term 3 B₁² V̂[β̂] times regularization, which keeps the bandwidth finite where the derivative is nearly zero (Imbens and Kalyanaraman 2012). When p - ν is even and interior = false, the bias includes the next term of its expansion and the mean squared error is minimized numerically. Its squared bias is then (|B₁| + h |B₂|)² plus the regularization term, which cannot vanish where the two estimated terms have opposite signs; nprobust's (B₁ + h B₂ + regularization R)², kept with compat = true, can, and since it adds R, which has the units of B₁², to B₁, its bandwidth depends on the units of y.

By default, the pilot bandwidth b is the CrossValidation bandwidth of the fit of order p times the ratio of the RuleOfThumb bandwidths for the derivative of order p + 1 and for the regression function, so that it adapts to the roughness of the regression function; the pilot for the derivative of order p + 2, which even p - ν needs, is formed in the same way. The derivative of order p + 1 is then estimated by a fit of order q with bandwidth b. Cross-validation assumes independent errors: with positively correlated errors it undersmooths, and so does the pilot. With compat = true, the rule is nprobust's lpbwselect with bwselect = "mse-dpi" instead, which agrees with nprobust 1.0.0 to rounding. Its pilot bandwidths come from three more plug-in stages whose bias constants are estimated by fits over the whole range of the data, and at interior points they end at the cap, so that the derivative is estimated over nearly the whole range. For local linear fits, its bandwidths are then about twice the optimal ones at a sharp peak and five times for sin(6πx), where the adaptive pilots come within 10 percent at interior points.

The selection holds one bandwidth per point, and the pilot bandwidths in details.b; fit with pilot = :selected to use them, since by default the fit uses b = h / rho, as nprobust does. With the adaptive pilots, details also holds the cross-validation bandwidth.

For a sample without responses, the rule gives the plug-in bandwidth for the density at each point, h = (R(k) f̂(x) / (4 n (B̂² + regularization R̂)))^(1/5) with B̂ = μ₂(k)/2 f̂''(x) and R̂ = 3 (μ₂(k)/2)² V̂[f̂''(x)]. The pilot f̂'' uses the bandwidth b, the normal-reference ratio of the bandwidths for f'' and for f applied to the SheatherJones bandwidth, so that it adapts to multimodal densities, and f̂ uses the Sheather–Jones bandwidth. With compat = true, it gives nprobust's kdbwselect instead, whose rule-of-thumb pilots assume a normal density, for the Epanechnikov and uniform kernels; for a bimodal density they can make the bandwidth in the valley ten times too large.

For LocalPolynomialDensity, the rule is the plug-in bandwidth of R's lpdensity (bwselect = "mse-dpi"), which minimizes the estimated asymptotic mean squared error a^(2p+2-2v) (B₁ + a B₂)² + σ²/a^(2v-1) at each point, where v = derivative + 1 is the order of the derivative of the distribution function. The derivatives of orders p + 1 and p + 2 in B₁ and B₂ come from local fits of orders p + 2 and p + 3 at rule-of-thumb bandwidths for the whole set of points, and the constants of the bias and the variance from the data at a third such bandwidth. The options regularization and interior do not apply there. Unless compat = true, the squared bias is bounded by (|B₁| + a |B₂|)², since lpdensity's (B₁ + a B₂)² vanishes at a = -B₁/B₂ when the estimated terms have opposite signs.
