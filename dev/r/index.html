Comparison with R

Most procedures of this package have a counterpart in an R package, and many users will come from one of them. In this page, we answer two questions: what agrees with R, and where the defaults differ and why. A table at the end translates the argument names.

Note that agreement with R is a property we test, not one we assume. For every counterpart below, the test suite compares the package with reference values that an R script in test/data generates. The script records the version of the R package it used, and the tolerances are those of the tests.

What agrees with R

With compat = true, where the keyword exists, the procedures reproduce the R packages, including their conventions for ties, mass points, leverages and clusters. DensityDiscontinuity and the honest intervals have no compat keyword, since they reproduce rddensity and RDHonest by default, with the exceptions listed in the next section.

Where agreement is not to rounding, the reason is the R package's own numerical accuracy, such as a numerical minimization or integration with a coarse tolerance, not a difference of method.

Where the defaults differ

The plug-in bandwidths are pointwise (MSEOptimal) at a single point and integrated (IMSEOptimal) at several, as in nprobust. In the following places the defaults depart from the R packages. Each departure fixes a behavior of the R package that we found to fail in a concrete case, and compat = true reproduces the R package where the keyword exists.

  1. The density plug-in of nprobust estimates $f''$ with a pilot bandwidth that assumes a normal density. For a bimodal density this pilot can be far too wide: in the valley of an equal mixture of $N(\pm 1, 1/4)$ with 2000 observations, the selected bandwidth was ten times the Sheather–Jones bandwidth. We scale the pilot from the Sheather–Jones bandwidth instead, and use the bias constant of Calonico, Cattaneo and Farrell (2018), while nprobust 1.0.0 removes only 5/9 of the leading bias for the Epanechnikov kernel.
  2. The regression plug-in of nprobust estimates the derivative in the bias with pilot bandwidths that, at interior points, end at their cap, the distance to the farther end of the data. Its bandwidths are then too large wherever the curvature is local, about twice the optimal ones at a sharp peak. We scale the pilot from the cross-validation bandwidth instead.
  3. The plug-in of lpdensity minimizes a mean squared error whose squared bias $(B_1 + a B_2)^2$ vanishes when the estimated terms have opposite signs, and it then chooses bandwidths of the order of the range of the data. We bound the bias by $(|B_1| + a |B_2|)^2$.
  4. The regression plug-in of nprobust has the same flaw for even $p - \nu$, whose squared bias $(B_1 + h B_2 + \lambda R)^2$ moreover adds the regularization $R$, which has the units of $B_1^2$, to $B_1$, so that the bandwidth depends on the units of $y$. We use $(|B_1| + h |B_2|)^2 + \lambda R$. Its integrated rule also leaves out the regularization for odd $p - \nu$ and has no upper bound, so that an exactly linear regression function on the unit interval gave a bandwidth of 966; we keep the regularization and cap the bandwidth at the range of the data.
  5. The floor of nprobust raises the bandwidths at each point to the distance of the 21st nearest observation. With heavy ties, such a window can hold fewer distinct values than the fit has coefficients, and the estimate is then undefined: with four distinct values of $x$, the default fit left 12 of its 30 estimates and all bias corrections NaN. We raise the bandwidths further where needed, so that the window of $h$ holds $p + 1$ distinct values with positive weight and that of the pilot $b$ holds $p + 2$.
  6. The nearest-neighbour variance estimator of nprobust takes the neighbours on both sides when they are at the same distance, and compares the two distances exactly. On an equally spaced design, rounding then decides which neighbours count, and a shift of the design changed the standard errors by up to 9 percent. We treat distances that agree to about eight digits as equal.
  7. The coverage-error optimal bandwidths of nprobust rescale the plug-in bandwidths to the rate that is optimal at the boundary, also at interior points, which shrinks local linear bandwidths by $n^{-1/20}$. For odd $p - \nu$ the optimal rate at interior points is that of the mean squared error, and CoverageOptimal uses it by default, so that it leaves local linear bandwidths unchanged.
  8. The bandwidth rules of lpdensity standardize the data by their mean and standard deviation: one outlier at $10^4$ among 2000 normal observations moved its rule of thumb at 0 from 0.55 to 124. We standardize by the median and a robust scale. Its pilot bandwidths are integrated over the evaluation points, so that one far point changes the bandwidths at all the others, and ours over the quantiles of the data. Its rule of thumb jumps towards the range of the data at the inflection points of the normal density, where the leading bias vanishes, and ours adds the next term of the bias.
  9. nprobust gives a standard error of zero where no observation has positive weight, lpdensity extrapolates to points far outside the data, and both let intervals for a density reach below zero. We return NaN in the first two cases and clamp the lower ends at zero.
  10. R's bw.SJ takes the smallest root of the equation of Sheather and Jones. With rounded data, pairs of equal values create roots at bandwidths that resolve the grid of the values: for 10^5 normal observations rounded to 0.1 it gave 0.0026, at which the estimate is a comb of spikes. SheatherJones searches only bandwidths that do not resolve the grid, and gives 0.106 there.
  11. nprobust evaluates regression by default at 30 equally spaced points between the smallest and the largest observation, and its integrated bandwidth averages over such points. For a covariate with heavy tails, most of them lie where few observations are left. We evaluate between the quantiles of order 0.025 and 0.975, and average over 30 quantiles of order 0.05 to 0.95.
  12. For clustered data, nprobust adjusts the residuals of the bias-corrected estimate for CR2 and CR3 with a matrix that is not a block of a hat matrix and changes with the units of $x$: at the boundary, measuring $x$ in other units changed these standard errors by five orders of magnitude. We adjust them with the hat matrix of the fit of order $p$, as for HC2 and HC3. Furthermore, nprobust's covariances between points ignore the clusters off the diagonal; ours sum over clusters also with compat = true.
  13. For regression discontinuity, rdrobust floors $1 - h_{ii}$ at a small constant for leverages near one, uses a factor of one for CR3, and falls back on other estimates for clusters whose block of the hat matrix is singular. By default, as for local polynomial regression, a leverage of one or a singular block leaves no residual to estimate the variance from, and the standard error is NaN, while CR3 has the factor $(G - 1)/G$.
  14. For the manipulation test, the results are NaN where the window of a side holds too few distinct values for the fit or its normal matrix is singular to working precision. The R package can return numbers that depend on rounding there. Furthermore, -0.0 and 0.0 count as one distinct value, as in R's unique(). The pilot fits of order $p + 2$ have ill-conditioned normal equations, which we sum with compensated arithmetic, so that the results agree with rddensity to about 1e-9, its own rounding error.
  15. The honest intervals agree with RDHonest by default. Its rule of thumb for $M$ needs 5 distinct values of $x$ on each side of the cutoff, and like RDHonest we throw an error below that.
  16. ks's two-stage plug-in matrix (Hpi, and Hpi.diag, with the pilot "samse") reads the estimated functionals of order 6 in the wrong order when it chooses the pilot of order 4: it passes the whole tensor of derivatives where the pilot expects the distinct functionals, so that, for example, $\psi_{51}$ takes the place of $\psi_{42}$ in two dimensions. For the sample of the tests, its pilot was 0.723 instead of 0.653, and the matrix differed by 5%. PlugInMatrix reads them in the right order, and compat = true reproduces ks.
  17. For $d \geq 3$, ks's plug-in and smoothed cross-validation matrices default to the pilot "dscalar" of Chacón and Duong (2010); PlugInMatrix uses the SAMSE pilot of Duong and Hazelton (2003) in every dimension, which is ks's default for $d = 2$. Furthermore, ks bins the data by default above 500 observations in two dimensions and above 1000 in three; our selectors sum over the pairs exactly.
  18. SCVMatrix agrees with ks's Hscv in its criterion, to rounding at a given pilot, but its pilot follows Theorem 4 of Duong (2004), from which ks's differs, by about 5% for the samples of the tests; the selected matrices differed by 2% to 3%. Least-squares cross-validation agrees with ks's Hlscv in two

dimensions; in three, for the sample of the tests, ks stopped at a matrix whose criterion was higher than at ours. For $d \geq 3$ ks's Hns.diag is not the diagonal matrix that minimizes the normal-reference error; NormalScaleMatrix(diagonal = true) is, and agrees with ks for $d = 2$.

  1. np's least-squares cross-validation for conditional densities (bwmethod = "cv.ls") reports a criterion that differs by about 0.6% from that of Hall, Racine and Li (2004) at its bandwidths, and its selection is not a minimum of that criterion. ConditionalCV(criterion = :leastsquares) minimizes the criterion of the paper.
