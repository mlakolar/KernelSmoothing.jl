KernelSmoothing.jl

Kernel density estimation and local polynomial regression, with data-driven bandwidth selection and robust bias-corrected inference. The package is under development.

It provides:

  • kernel density estimates that are distributions of Distributions.jl, robust bias-corrected intervals for densities at interior points, and the local polynomial density estimator of Cattaneo, Jansson and Ma for densities with a boundary;
  • local polynomial regression of a regression function or its derivatives, with conventional, robust bias-corrected and honest intervals, covariances between points and uniform bands;
  • bandwidth selectors: rules of thumb, Sheather and Jones, cross-validation and plug-in bandwidths.

The estimators are built in layers. Kernels are the smoothing kernels with their distribution functions, moments, derivatives, convolutions and products. Prepared data is a sample sorted once, with its weights and ties recorded. Kernel sums are the sums $\sum_i w_i z_i K(u_i) u_i^r$ that all estimators are built from, computed by exact direct, windowed and fast engines or approximately from binned data.

KernelSmoothing

Kernel density estimation and local polynomial regression, with data-driven bandwidth selection and robust bias-corrected inference.

The estimators are KDE, a kernel density estimate that is a distribution of Distributions.jl; PointwiseDensity, density estimates at a set of points with robust bias-corrected intervals; LocalPolynomialDensity, the boundary-adaptive density estimator of Cattaneo, Jansson and Ma; and LocalPolynomialRegression, local polynomial estimates of a regression function or its derivatives with conventional, robust bias-corrected and Honest intervals and uniform bands, see confband. They are fitted with fit and choose their bandwidths with a BandwidthSelector. Underneath are the smoothing kernels, see AbstractKernel, samples prepared for smoothing, see SmoothingData, and the kernel sums that many of the estimators and bandwidth selectors are built from, see kernelsums.

Example

using KernelSmoothing

x = randn(1000)
f = fit(KDE, x)                      # Epanechnikov kernel, Sheather–Jones bandwidth
pdf(f, 0.0), cdf(f, 0.0), quantile(f, 0.975)
pdf.(f, range(-3, 3; length = 401))  # many points at once, exactly
derivative(f, 1)(0.5)                # the estimated derivative of the density
confint(fit(PointwiseDensity, x; points = [-1, 0, 1]))   # robust bias-corrected intervals

t = rand(2000); y = sin.(2π .* t) .+ 0.3 .* randn(2000)
m = fit(LocalPolynomialRegression, t, y; points = range(0, 1; length = 21))
coef(m), confint(m)                  # estimates and robust bias-corrected intervals
confband(m)                          # a uniform band over the 21 points

Defaults and agreement with R

The estimators follow R's nprobust, lpdensity and RDHonest closely. With given bandwidths, the estimates and standard errors agree with these packages to rounding, as the tests check against reference values from them. The defaults are the following.

estimatorkernelbandwidthcompat = true gives
fit(KDE, x)EpanechnikovSheatherJonesbandwidth = SheatherJones(compat = true): R's bw.SJ
fit(PointwiseDensity, x)Epanechnikovplug-in, pilots anchored on Sheather–Jonesnprobust's kdrobust
fit(LocalPolynomialRegression, x, y)Epanechnikov, degree derivative + 1plug-in, pilots anchored on cross-validationnprobust's lprobust
fit(LocalPolynomialDensity, x)triangular, degree 2lpdensity's plug-in, with a bound on the biaslpdensity

The plug-in bandwidths are pointwise (MSEOptimal) at a single point and integrated (IMSEOptimal) at several, as in nprobust. In the following places the defaults depart from the R packages, which compat = true reproduces:

  1. The density plug-in of nprobust estimates $f''$ with a pilot bandwidth that assumes a normal density. For a bimodal density this pilot can be far too wide: in the valley of an equal mixture of $N(\pm 1, 1/4)$ with 2000 observations, the selected bandwidth was ten times the Sheather–Jones bandwidth. We scale the pilot from the Sheather–Jones bandwidth instead, and use the bias constant of Calonico, Cattaneo and Farrell (2018), while nprobust 1.0.0 removes only 5/9 of the leading bias for the Epanechnikov kernel.
  2. The regression plug-in of nprobust estimates the derivative in the bias with pilot bandwidths that, at interior points, end at their cap, the distance to the farther end of the data. Its bandwidths are then too large wherever the curvature is local, about twice the optimal ones at a sharp peak. We scale the pilot from the cross-validation bandwidth instead.
  3. The plug-in of lpdensity minimizes a mean squared error whose squared bias $(B_1 + a B_2)^2$ vanishes when the estimated terms have opposite signs, and it then chooses bandwidths of the order of the range of the data. We bound the bias by $(|B_1| + a |B_2|)^2$.
  4. The regression plug-in of nprobust has the same flaw for even $p - \nu$, whose squared bias $(B_1 + h B_2 + \lambda R)^2$ moreover adds the regularization $R$, which has the units of $B_1^2$, to $B_1$, so that the bandwidth depends on the units of $y$. We use $(|B_1| + h |B_2|)^2 + \lambda R$. Its integrated rule also leaves out the regularization for odd $p - \nu$ and has no upper bound, so that an exactly linear regression function on the unit interval gave a bandwidth of 966; we keep the regularization and cap the bandwidth at the range of the data.
  5. The floor of nprobust raises the bandwidths at each point to the distance of the 21st nearest observation. With heavy ties, such a window can hold fewer distinct values than the fit has coefficients, and the estimate is then undefined: with four distinct values of $x$, the default fit left 12 of its 30 estimates and all bias corrections NaN. We raise the bandwidths further where needed, so that the window of $h$ holds $p + 1$ distinct values with positive weight and that of the pilot $b$ holds $p + 2$.
  6. The nearest-neighbour variance estimator of nprobust takes the neighbours on both sides when they are at the same distance, and compares the two distances exactly. On an equally spaced design, rounding then decides which neighbours count, and a shift of the design changed the standard errors by up to 9 percent. We treat distances that agree to about eight digits as equal.
  7. The coverage-error optimal bandwidths of nprobust rescale the plug-in bandwidths to the rate that is optimal at the boundary, also at interior points, which shrinks local linear bandwidths by $n^{-1/20}$. For odd $p - \nu$ the optimal rate at interior points is that of the mean squared error, and CoverageOptimal uses it by default, so that it leaves local linear bandwidths unchanged.
  8. The bandwidth rules of lpdensity standardize the data by their mean and standard deviation: one outlier at $10^4$ among 2000 normal observations moved its rule of thumb at 0 from 0.55 to 124. We standardize by the median and a robust scale. Its pilot bandwidths are integrated over the evaluation points, so that one far point changes the bandwidths at all the others, and ours over the quantiles of the data. Its rule of thumb jumps towards the range of the data at the inflection points of the normal density, where the leading bias vanishes, and ours adds the next term of the bias.
  9. nprobust gives a standard error of zero where no observation has positive weight, lpdensity extrapolates to points far outside the data, and both let intervals for a density reach below zero. We return NaN in the first two cases and clamp the lower ends at zero.
  10. R's bw.SJ takes the smallest root of the equation of Sheather and Jones. With rounded data, pairs of equal values create roots at bandwidths that resolve the grid of the values: for 10^5 normal observations rounded to 0.1 it gave 0.0026, at which the estimate is a comb of spikes. SheatherJones searches only bandwidths that do not resolve the grid, and gives 0.106 there.

The pages of the estimators give the details.

For all fits, coef(m) and stderror(m) describe the conventional estimates, and coef(m; method = RobustBiasCorrected()) and stderror(m; method = RobustBiasCorrected()) give the bias-corrected estimates and their robust standard errors. The intervals of confint(m) and the bands of confband(m) are robust bias-corrected by default and centred at the bias-corrected estimates, since conventional intervals cover only for bandwidths well below the ones that minimize the mean squared error. coeftable(m) lists both.

Bandwidth convention

The compactly supported kernels live on [-1, 1], so the bandwidth h is the half-width of the smoothing window: an observation gets weight zero unless it is within h of the point. The Gaussian kernel is the standard normal density, so its bandwidth is a standard deviation. KernelDensity.jl, KDEpy and R's density instead scale every kernel to unit variance; their bandwidth h_sd corresponds to the half-width h_sd / sqrt(moment(k, 2)) here, for example h_sd √5 for the Epanechnikov kernel.

Threads

Kernel sums, fits at several points and cross-validation run in parallel when Julia is started with several threads, for example with julia --threads=auto. The results do not depend on the number of threads.
